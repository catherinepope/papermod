Even with good habits in place, things still go wrong. Experiments produce unexpected results, grants get rejected, and manuscripts are returned with endless revisions. As [the Circle of Control](/workshops/resources/your-circle-of-control/) shows, we can manage our inputs but not always the outcomes.

A 100% success rate *sounds* attractive, but it would be boring. As Margaret Heffernan points out in [_Embracing Uncertainty_](https://uk.bookshop.org/a/2760/9781447372677): “When attempting something new, most organisations demand proof that the plan will work before it’s begun. But, of course, if the proof existed, the innovation wouldn’t be innovation.” If you could guarantee that everything would work, you would just be repeating what's out there already.

If you're trying something new, failure is inevitable. And failure isn't always bad. Sometimes it leads to breakthroughs, or at least useful insights. In *The Right Kind of Wrong*, Amy Edmondson explains that “Good failures are those that bring us valuable information that simply *could not have been gained any other way*.” Edmondson categorises three types of failure:

- **Basic failures** are caused by mistakes. They are the most easily understood and can be avoided through improved focus and clear plans.
- **Complex failures** have multiple causes and are sometimes exacerbated by bad luck. They are often averted by dealing with small problems or basic failures before they become bigger.
- **Intelligent failures**, or good failures, are necessary for progress. They lead to discoveries, or at least a narrowing of the problem space.

Rather than yelling at ourselves when things go wrong, it’s important to learn from the situation. This is hard to do when we’re thinking judgementally, rather than analytically.

When it comes to motivation, self-care is more effective than self-criticism. Kristin Neff encourages us to practice self-*compassion*. This involves:

- Kindness to yourself.
- Seeing failure as part of the shared human experience.
- Mindfulness, rather than over-identification.

Self-compassion isn’t self-indulgence or lowering your standards, it’s about creating the psychological safety to take risks, learn from failure, and try again. The Colleague Test is a useful device. If a colleague came you having experienced the same 'failure', how would you respond to them? You would almost certainly be kind, compassionate, and encouraging, helping them put it in perspective and reminding them of previous successes. We tend to be much harsher critics of ourselves that of other people. Of course, it’s better that way around, but we should still apply consistent standards to everyone — including ourselves.
